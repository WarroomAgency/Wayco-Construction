Design-build puts design and construction under one contract with one accountable team. Design-bid-build separates them: you hire a designer, finish the plans, then collect bids from contractors. For most homeowners and small commercial owners, design-build offers simpler communication and earlier cost feedback. Design-bid-build suits owners who already have complete plans.

## What is design-build?

In a design-build project, a single company, such as Wayco Construction, handles planning, design, engineering coordination, permitting, and construction. You sign one agreement and work with one team. The builder is part of the conversation from the first sketch, so costs, schedule, and buildability are checked as the design develops.

That is how we run [design-build](/design-build) projects. It is also why most Wayco projects can start within about four weeks, with design and submittals first, then procurement, then mobilization.

## What is design-bid-build?

Design-bid-build is the traditional sequence. You hire an architect or designer, who produces a complete set of plans and specifications. Those plans go out to several contractors for bids. You select a contractor, and construction begins. The designer and the builder are separate parties with separate contracts.

This model has a long history and can work well, especially on public projects where competitive bidding is required. It also asks more of the owner, who coordinates between design and construction teams.

## How do the two approaches compare?

| | Design-build | Design-bid-build |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contracts | One agreement | Separate design and construction contracts |
| Accountability | One team | Split between designer and builder |
| Cost feedback | During design | After plans are complete |
| Schedule | Design and procurement overlap | Phases happen in sequence |
| Owner involvement | Lower coordination load | Higher coordination load |
| Bidding | Typically a selected team | Competitive bids on a defined scope |
| Change handling | Builder aware of design intent | Plan gaps can lead to disputes |

Neither is always right. The question is where the risk and effort should sit.

## Why do many owners choose design-build?

### One point of accountability

If something does not fit, there is no finger pointing between designer and builder. One team owns the result. That matters when a wall is not where the drawing said it would be, or when a detail turns out harder to build than it looked.

### Earlier, more realistic pricing

When the builder is at the table during design, you hear what each choice costs while you can still change it. That is the core of value engineering: finding smart ways to deliver what you want for less, before the plans are final. Designing a plan you cannot afford and then redrawing it is a common and expensive detour.

### A faster path to construction

Because design, submittals, and procurement overlap, you do not wait for a full set of drawings before ordering long lead items. Faster is not rushed. It simply avoids dead time.

### Less coordination for you

Owners often underestimate how much time design-bid-build asks of them. Someone has to reconcile questions between the architect, engineers, contractors, and the city or county. In design-build, we do that.

## When does design-bid-build make sense?

It can be the right fit if:

- You already have a complete set of plans from an architect you trust.
- You want to compare several bids on an identical scope.
- A public or institutional rule requires competitive bidding.
- The project is highly specialized and the designer needs independence from the builder.

If you go this route, invest in complete, coordinated plans. Gaps in drawings are where change orders come from.

## How do you decide?

Ask these questions.

1. **Do you already have finished plans?** If yes, bidding may work. If not, design-build helps you build plans around a budget.
2. **How much time do you want to spend coordinating?** Less time points to design-build.
3. **How important is cost certainty early?** Design-build gives you feedback sooner.
4. **Is competitive bidding required?** If so, you may have no choice.
5. **What scale and complexity is the project?** Many residential and tenant-improvement projects are a natural fit for design-build.

## What are the risks of each approach?

Every delivery method has weak points. In design-build, the risk is choosing a team without checking whether they listen. Ask how decisions are documented, how pricing is shared during design, and how you approve changes. A good design-build firm shows its numbers early.

In design-bid-build, the risk lives in the gaps. If the plans are incomplete or unclear, contractors price their assumptions, and disagreements show up later as change orders. The designer may also draw something that exceeds your budget, and you will not find out until bids arrive. Neither risk is a reason to avoid the method. They are reasons to ask better questions up front.
