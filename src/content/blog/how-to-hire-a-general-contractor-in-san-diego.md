To hire a general contractor in San Diego, verify the license with the California Contractors State License Board (CSLB), compare written scopes rather than just prices, check references and recent work, confirm who handles permits, and get everything in a written contract. Ask how they communicate, what the warranty covers, and how changes are handled.

## How do you verify a contractor's license?

Verify any contractor's license with the California Contractors State License Board (CSLB). Their website lets you look up a contractor by name or license number. Check that:

- The license is active and in good standing
- The classification matches the work you are hiring for
- Required bonds and workers' compensation information are on file
- There is no disciplinary history that concerns you

Do this yourself, even if the contractor gives you a number. It takes a few minutes and is the single most useful step you can take.

## What questions should you ask a contractor?

Here are ten that separate prepared contractors from the rest.

1. **Who will be my point of contact, and how often will we talk?**
2. **Who handles permits, and what do you do when a plan check comes back with comments?**
3. **Can I see recent projects similar to mine and talk to past clients?**
4. **Who will actually be on site every day, and who supervises them?**
5. **What is included in your price, and what is not?**
6. **How are changes priced and approved?**
7. **What is the payment schedule, and what milestones trigger payments?**
8. **What is the start date, the estimated duration, and what could affect it?**
9. **What warranty do you offer, and what is the process if something fails?**
10. **What insurance do you carry, and can I see a certificate?**

## What does a good estimate look like?

A useful estimate reads like a plan. Compare these two styles.

| A weak estimate | A strong estimate |
| --- | --- |
| One lump sum | Itemized scope by phase or trade |
| "Materials included" | Allowances and specific materials listed |
| No exclusions | Clear inclusions and exclusions |
| No schedule | Start window and estimated duration |
| Verbal promises | Everything in writing |

If two bids differ by a lot, ask why. The difference is almost always scope, not magic.

## What are the red flags when hiring a contractor?

- Pressure to sign today or "special pricing" that expires
- A large upfront deposit well beyond what is typical for your project
- No written contract, or one that is vague about scope
- Reluctance to provide a license number or insurance proof
- No references, no portfolio, or no physical business address
- A bid far below all others
- Cash-only requests or requests to pull permits in your name when they should be handling them
- Poor communication before you have even hired them

Your first interactions are a preview. If a contractor is slow to return calls and sloppy with details before you sign, expect more of the same after.

## How do you check references and reviews?

Read online reviews with an eye for patterns, not star ratings alone. Look for comments about communication, schedule, how problems were handled, and how the warranty was honored. Ask for two or three recent references with similar projects, and ask them what went wrong and how it was resolved. Every project has bumps. What matters is the response.

## What should be in the contract?

- Detailed scope of work and plans
- Total price and payment schedule
- Start and estimated completion windows
- Who obtains permits and schedules inspections
- Change-order process
- Warranty terms
- Insurance and licensing information
- How disputes are handled

For larger projects, consider an attorney review. Contract details vary, and this article is general guidance, not legal advice.

## What is the difference between a general contractor and a design-build contractor?

A general contractor builds from plans and manages subcontractors. A design-build firm also handles design and planning, so the same team takes you from concept to completion. Both can be good, but design-build simplifies communication. ## How do you trust your gut without ignoring the facts?

Paperwork matters, but so does how a contractor treats you. Notice whether they listen to your goals, explain tradeoffs in plain language, and say no when something is a bad idea. A contractor who agrees with everything is not protecting your project.

Look at how they handle the first meeting. Did they show up on time? Did they ask about your budget, your timeline, and how you use the space? Did they follow up when they said they would? Those small behaviors predict how the project will feel on day thirty. Combine that read with a verified license, written scopes, and references, and you will have a well-rounded view.
