Phase a whole-home renovation from the structure outward: first structural and permit items, then roof and exterior envelope, then rough plumbing, electrical, and HVAC, and finally insulation, drywall, and finishes. Finishes go last because every upstream trade can damage them. Done in the right order, you pay for each task once.

## Why does the order of work matter so much?

Houses are layered. Framing is behind insulation, insulation is behind drywall, drywall is behind paint and cabinets. If you install a beautiful floor and then open walls for new wiring, you will repair the floor. A phasing plan exists to prevent that kind of rework.

It also helps with money. Knowing what must happen first lets you decide what to spend now and what to hold for later.

## What comes first: planning, permits, and surprises?

Before any demolition, spend time on three things.

### Define the scope

Walk through the house room by room and write down what you want, what you need, and what you can live without. Separate "must do now" from "nice to have later." Our [design and planning](/design-and-planning) process turns that list into drawings and a written scope.

### Handle permits and engineering

Structural changes, new openings, electrical service upgrades, and plumbing relocations generally require permits and sometimes engineering. Requirements vary by city and county. Wayco handles all required permitting, so you are not coordinating with agencies yourself.

### Plan for hidden conditions

Older homes especially can hide outdated wiring, plumbing, or framing issues. A contingency in your budget is not pessimism. It is how projects stay on track when walls come open.

## What is the typical sequence for a whole-home renovation?

| Phase | Typical work |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Planning and permits | Scope, drawings, engineering, permit submittals |
| 2. Demolition and structure | Selective demo, framing changes, foundation or structural repairs |
| 3. Envelope | Roofing, windows, exterior doors, siding or stucco |
| 4. Rough systems | Plumbing, electrical, HVAC, low-voltage |
| 5. Inspections and insulation | Required inspections, insulation, air sealing |
| 6. Drywall and prep | Hang, tape, texture, prime |
| 7. Finishes | Cabinets, tile, flooring, trim, paint, fixtures |
| 8. Closeout | Final inspections, punch list, walkthrough |

The details shift from house to house, but the logic holds: build the shell, run the systems, close the walls, then finish.

## Should you renovate all at once or in phases?

Both approaches work. Choose based on your situation.

**All at once** usually means one mobilization, one permit package, shared trades working in sequence, and a shorter total disruption. It is often the most efficient path if you can leave the home or handle the disruption.

**In phases** spreads cost and can let you stay in the house. The risk is paying for some work twice, such as opening a wall again or repainting an area a second time. If you phase, build a master plan first so that each phase fits the whole.

Consider phasing by area rather than by trade: for example, the kitchen and main living areas first, then bedrooms and baths later. Rough systems that serve the entire house, such as an electrical panel upgrade or HVAC, are often best done early even when finishes are delayed.

## What should you do in the first phase if you can't do everything?

If budget forces choices, prioritize in this order:

1. Safety and structure: foundation, framing, electrical, roof leaks
2. Systems that will be hard to reach later: panels, plumbing, ducting
3. Envelope: windows, doors, insulation, exterior
4. High-use rooms: kitchen and primary bath
5. Cosmetic finishes in lower-use rooms

Things like paint, trim, and decorative lighting are easiest to do last and easiest to change later.

## How do you live at home during a renovation?

- Pick a "safe zone," a room or two that stays untouched.
- Plan for water, power, and kitchen outages and ask for a schedule of those days.
- Expect dust and noise, and agree on work hours.
- Protect furniture and air quality with containment and floor protection.
- Keep a short weekly check-in with your project contact so surprises stay small.

## How do you budget a phased renovation?

Build the budget around the master plan, not around individual phases. Start by pricing the entire scope, then assign each part to a phase. This shows you the true total and prevents the common trap of spending the budget on the kitchen and having nothing left for the roof or the electrical panel.

Keep a reserve in every phase for hidden conditions, and revisit the plan after each phase is complete. Prices, products, and your own priorities will shift over time. A clear master plan lets you adjust without tearing up finished work. Ask your contractor for itemized pricing by area so you can move items between phases with confidence.
