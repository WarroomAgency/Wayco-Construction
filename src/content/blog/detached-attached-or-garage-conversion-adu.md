The best ADU type depends on your lot, your budget, and how you plan to use the space. A detached ADU gives the most privacy, an attached ADU can fit tighter lots, and a garage conversion often costs the least when the existing structure is sound. Local rules in your San Diego County city or unincorporated area still apply.

## What are the three main ADU types?

Accessory dwelling units come in three common forms. Each solves a different problem.

- **Detached ADU:** a separate, standalone building, usually in the backyard.
- **Attached ADU:** a unit that shares at least one wall with the main house, such as a side or rear addition.
- **Garage conversion:** an existing garage turned into a living space.

California law generally allows ADUs on residential lots, and the state has made approvals more predictable in recent years. Details such as height, setbacks, and parking still vary by city and county, so check your local rules or let us do it with you during design.

## How do the three ADU types compare?

| | Detached | Attached | Garage conversion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Privacy | Highest | Moderate | Moderate to high |
| Typical cost | Higher | Middle | Often lowest |
| Build complexity | New foundation and structure | Tie-in to existing house | Reuses existing shell |
| Lot needed | Open yard area | Space beside or behind home | Existing garage |
| Best for | Rental, guests, long-term flexibility | Family or caregiving | Budget-conscious projects |

These are general patterns, not guarantees. A well-situated detached ADU can cost less than a difficult conversion, and a sound garage can be the easiest project on the block.

## When is a detached ADU the right choice?

A detached ADU is the closest thing to a second home on your property. It works well when you have a usable backyard, want separation from the main house, and plan to rent it or host family long term.

The tradeoff is cost and site work: you need a new foundation, framing, roof, and utility connections. [Our cost guide](/blog/how-much-does-an-adu-cost-in-san-diego) explains what moves the number.

## When does an attached ADU make sense?

An attached ADU suits owners who want to keep a unit close, such as for an aging parent or an adult child. Sharing a wall can simplify access to power, water, and sewer. It also means sound, fire separation, and structural tie-ins need careful design.

If your lot is narrow or your backyard is occupied by a pool, trees, or slope, an addition may fit where a freestanding building will not. Our [home additions](/home-additions) team handles that kind of work too.

## When should you convert a garage into an ADU?

A garage conversion is attractive when you already have the footprint and want to keep costs in check. Before you commit, we check a few things:

- The condition of the slab, including moisture and slope
- Framing, roof, and wall assemblies
- Ceiling height and insulation
- Where electrical, plumbing, and sewer will connect
- Parking requirements in your jurisdiction
- What happens to your storage and vehicles

Parking is where owners are often surprised. Rules differ by city and county, and replacing parking can change the project. Ask your jurisdiction early.

## How do you choose between the three?

Ask yourself these questions, in order.

1. **Who will live there?** Family needs close access. Tenants often prefer separation.
2. **What does your lot allow?** Setbacks, easements, trees, slope, and utilities decide a lot.
3. **What is your budget?** Start with the likely range, then see which type fits.
4. **How will you use it in ten years?** Flexibility matters.
5. **What do you need to keep?** If the garage is your workshop or parking, a conversion has real costs.

A site visit answers most of these in an hour. We walk the property, look at your garage or yard, and tell you plainly which options are realistic.

## What should you ask about your own lot before choosing?

A few lot-specific facts can settle the question quickly. Gather them before the first meeting and the conversation gets much more useful.

- A copy of your property survey or plot plan, if you have one
- The location of existing sewer, water, gas, and electrical connections
- Any easements, slopes, large trees, or retaining walls in the backyard
- The size and condition of your garage, including the slab and roof
- Your HOA rules, if you belong to one, since they may add requirements

None of these will shut the door on an ADU by themselves. They simply tell us which type will cost less and go smoother on your particular property.
