A tenant improvement, or TI, is construction that adapts a leased commercial space to your business: walls, ceilings, lighting, plumbing, electrical, and finishes. San Diego business owners should review the lease, confirm who pays and who pulls permits, and hire a contractor early, because design, approvals, and long lead items shape the opening date.

## What counts as a tenant improvement?

A TI can be modest or extensive. Typical scopes include:

- Framing and drywall for new offices, exam rooms, or treatment areas
- Ceilings, lighting, and electrical for the new layout
- Plumbing for restrooms, break rooms, or specialty rooms
- HVAC adjustments for the new floor plan
- Flooring, paint, and millwork
- Storefront and signage coordination
- Data, security, and low-voltage rough-in

Our [tenant improvement](/tenant-improvement) service covers these scopes for offices, retail, restaurants, healthcare, and hospitality spaces.

## Who pays for tenant improvements?

This is a lease question, not a construction question, and it deserves attention before you sign. Common arrangements include:

- **Tenant allowance:** the landlord contributes a per-square-foot amount, and the tenant pays the rest.
- **Turnkey delivery:** the landlord builds out the space to an agreed standard.
- **As-is:** the tenant pays for everything.

Have your attorney or broker review who owns the improvements at lease end, whether you must remove them, and who is responsible for permits and insurance. We do not give legal advice, but we can give you a realistic scope and schedule to bring to the negotiation.

## How does the TI process work?

| Step | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Space evaluation | Walk the space, review the lease and building rules |
| 2. Programming | Define rooms, equipment, and workflow |
| 3. Design and planning | Layout, specifications, engineering coordination |
| 4. Permits | Submittals to the city or county; handled by your contractor |
| 5. Procurement | Order materials and long lead items |
| 6. Construction | Demolition, rough work, inspections, finishes |
| 7. Closeout | Final inspections, punch list, handover |

## What should you check in the lease and building rules?

Landlords often have specific requirements. Before design, ask for:

- Approved contractor lists or insurance requirements
- Building rules for work hours, loading, and elevator use
- Structural or utility limits, such as electrical capacity
- Signage and storefront rules
- Rules about roof and mechanical access

Ignoring building rules is a common way to lose weeks.

## How should you plan the schedule around your opening date?

Work backward from the date you need to be open, and then add margin. A TI schedule is a chain: lease signed, design finalized, permits issued, materials delivered, construction, inspections, move-in. Delay in one link moves the whole thing.

- Sign the lease only after you have a rough scope and schedule from a contractor.
- Start design as early as possible, even before the lease is final if the landlord allows it.
- Confirm lead times for equipment, millwork, and specialty finishes.
- Build in time for inspections and any required corrections.
- Keep your existing space until your new space is ready to occupy.

## How do you keep a TI on budget?

1. Define your must-haves and nice-to-haves before pricing.
2. Ask for itemized pricing so you can see where money goes.
3. Understand allowances and what happens if you exceed them.
4. Use value engineering to look for savings without hurting function.
5. Agree on a change-order process in writing.
