A bathroom remodel lasts when the hidden work is done right: a continuous waterproofing system behind the tile, a shower floor that slopes to the drain, a properly vented exhaust fan, and carefully set fixtures and glass. Surface finishes get the attention, but these details decide whether the bathroom looks good in ten years.

## What should you know about bathroom waterproofing?

Tile is a finish, not a barrier. Water moves through grout joints and tile edges over time, so the wall and floor assemblies behind them must be waterproof. This is the single most common cause of failed showers, and it is invisible until something goes wrong.

Good practice generally includes:

- A solid substrate that is flat and properly fastened
- A waterproofing membrane, either sheet or liquid-applied, installed per the manufacturer's instructions
- Sealed seams, corners, niches, and penetrations
- Proper overlap where the wall membrane meets the shower floor
- A flood test of the shower pan before tile goes in, where the system allows

Ask your contractor what system they use and how they test it. A confident answer is a good sign.

## How should a shower floor be sloped?

Water needs somewhere to go. A shower floor should slope toward the drain, commonly around a quarter inch per foot, though code and manufacturer requirements govern. Too little slope leaves puddles, and too much makes small tile hard to set cleanly.

Slope applies elsewhere too. The curb, bench tops, and niche bottoms should all pitch slightly toward the shower interior so water returns to the drain instead of sitting.

## What about tile selection and prep?

Tile choice affects both look and labor.

### Large-format vs. small tile

Large tiles mean fewer grout lines and a cleaner look, but they need a very flat substrate. Small tiles and mosaics conform to slopes and curves, which is why they are common on shower floors.

### Substrate and layout

Flatness matters more than most owners realize. Layout planning, which starts at a visible focal point and balances cuts at the edges, takes time but avoids awkward slivers. Your installer should show you the layout before the first tile goes up.

### Grout and sealing

Cementitious grout generally needs sealing, while other grout types have different care needs. Ask which product is used and how to maintain it. We work with showroom partners including MSI, Bedrosians Tile & Stone, and Daltile, and our showroom is open by appointment.

## Why does bathroom ventilation matter?

Moisture is the enemy. A bathroom without good ventilation grows mold, peels paint, and eventually damages framing.

- Use an exhaust fan sized for the room, and choose a quiet one so people actually turn it on.
- Vent to the exterior, not into the attic or a wall cavity.
- Use properly sized, insulated duct where needed, with as few bends as possible.
- Consider a humidity sensor or timer so the fan runs long enough after showers.

Code requirements vary, so we confirm them for your project during planning.

## How do you choose shower glass, fixtures, and hardware?

Glass is typically ordered after the shower is built, because the glass company measures the finished space. That means a lead time you should plan around. Frameless glass looks clean but needs precise walls and curbs.

Fixtures are where lead times and plumbing details collide. Pick the valve and trim early, since the valve goes in behind the wall before tile. Changing a valve after tile is a repair, not a swap.

| Item | When to decide | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shower valve and trim | Before rough plumbing | Valve sits behind the wall |
| Tile and layout | Before ordering | Lead time and quantities |
| Exhaust fan | Before rough electrical | Duct route and wiring |
| Shower glass | Order after walls are built | Measured to finished space |
| Vanity and faucet | Before rough plumbing | Drain and supply locations |

## What does a good bathroom remodel sequence look like?

1. Demolition and inspection of what is behind the walls
2. Repairs to framing or subfloor if needed
3. Rough plumbing and electrical
4. Inspections as required by your jurisdiction
5. Waterproofing and flood test
6. Tile and grout
7. Fixtures, vanity, lighting, and glass
8. Final clean, walk-through, and punch list

## What do homeowners often overlook?

Storage, lighting, and outlets. A beautiful bathroom with no place to put towels is frustrating. Think about where a hairdryer plugs in, how the mirror is lit, and whether a niche is at a usable height. Accessibility matters too: curbless showers, grab-bar blocking behind walls, and wider doors cost little when planned early and a lot to add later.

## What should you ask your contractor about the hidden work?

Most of what makes a bathroom last is behind the tile, so ask to see it. A professional will be glad to show you photos of the waterproofing before tile goes up, and to explain which system they are using and why. Ask about the flood test, how long the water stays in the pan, and how they handle corners, niches, and the transition between the wall and the floor.

Also ask about the substrate behind the tile, how the exhaust fan will be ducted, and what happens if rot or damage turns up when the old shower comes out. Surprises are common in older bathrooms. A plan for them is a sign of experience.
